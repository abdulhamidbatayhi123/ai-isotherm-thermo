# Introduction

Water sorbents built from metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) and covalent organic frameworks (COFs) are studied for atmospheric water harvesting [C14, doi:10.3390/en14123586; C43, doi:10.1021/jacs.9b13094] and for adsorption heat pumps and chillers [C15, doi:10.1002/ejic.201101056; C60, doi:10.1038/s41467-019-10960-0]. These devices run between temperatures: a MOF-801 cooling cycle adsorbs at 30 °C and regenerates at 80 to 85 °C [C34, doi:10.1016/j.enconman.2018.08.032]. Its design needs the uptake at temperatures where no isotherm was measured, and the isosteric heat, which enters the cycle model [C60, doi:10.1038/s41467-019-10960-0]. Both rest on few temperatures. A practical guide asks for isotherms spaced by 20 K or less for a Clausius-Clapeyron heat and advises against extrapolation beyond the measured range [C06, doi:10.1039/d0dt01784a]. For Mg-SU-102, isotherms were measured from 15 to 55 °C, but only those from 25 to 40 °C had enough points for the heat [C33, doi:10.1038/s41467-025-59551-2].

The standard temperature model for water in MOFs is the characteristic curve: the uptake is written as a function of the adsorption potential A = RT ln(p_sat/p), the isotherms of all temperatures are expected to fall on one curve, and that curve is used to model cycles [C11, doi:10.1021/acs.chemrev.9b00746]. Within this approach the isosteric heat is the vaporisation enthalpy plus A [C60, doi:10.1038/s41467-019-10960-0]. Invariance is usually shown by overlaying curves, without an error number, as for MIL-160 from 20 to 50 °C [C14, doi:10.3390/en14123586] and Al-fumarate from 298 to 333 K [C35, arXiv:2111.09602]. The Mg-SU-102 curves shift slightly towards lower energies as the temperature rises [C33, doi:10.1038/s41467-025-59551-2], and MOF-303 loses a significant part of its capacity between 25 and 45 °C [C59, doi:10.1021/acs.jpcc.4c01733]. Statistical thermodynamics gives a characteristic curve when added sorbate contributes purely energetically, and a temperature-dependent isotherm when sorbate number correlates with entropy [C26, doi:10.1021/acs.langmuir.1c01576]. The other common route, Langmuir or Sips sites with temperature-dependent affinities fitted to all temperatures at once [C60, doi:10.1038/s41467-019-10960-0; C07, doi:10.1002/aic.17186], has its own defects. Sips and Freundlich terms have no finite slope at zero pressure and their heats diverge at low coverage, dual-site Langmuir heats can show an unphysical maximum, and models that fit equally well disagree on the heat wherever they extrapolate [C07, doi:10.1002/aic.17186].

Machine-learned isotherm models add flexibility but little thermodynamics. In two recent examples temperature is a plain input: one network predicted methane isotherms of 17,644 MOFs from 278 to 338 K and was extrapolated to 268 and 358 K [L24, doi:10.1021/acs.jpcb.5c05384]; another predicts water isotherms from pressure and structural properties [L30, doi:10.1039/d3ta03586g]. Where heats appear, they are separate outputs. A neural ordinary differential equation learns dq/d ln p at one temperature, observes monotonicity without imposing it, and predicts the heat with a second network [L01, doi:10.1038/s41524-025-01819-8]. A zeolite study joined a learned loading at 300 K and an independently learned heat through an integrated Clausius-Clapeyron step with a temperature-independent heat [L04, doi:10.1021/acs.jpcc.4c00066]. When physics enters, it enters as a penalty. A physics-informed network for hydrogen sorption adds monotonicity as a weighted loss term and reports 98.6 % monotonicity satisfaction, so 1.4 % of held-out cases violate it [L02, doi:10.48550/arXiv.2603.28328]. A penalty-trained network is not provably monotone; monotonicity can only be certified afterwards [L19, arXiv:2011.10219].

A different construction is possible. The grand (immersion) potential of the adsorbed phase, Ω = −RT ∫ n d ln p, has the isotherm as its derivative with respect to ln p [C22, doi:10.1002/aic.690480115; C58, doi:10.1021/ja805129c]. A model can learn this one scalar as a function of ln p and T. If it is convex in ln p, the isotherm is monotone for every parameter value, and input-convex networks give convexity by construction [L15, arXiv:1609.07152]. A potential linear in p at low pressure gives a finite Henry slope, which Sips terms lack [C07, doi:10.1002/aic.17186]. The isosteric heat then follows by Clausius-Clapeyron from second derivatives of the same function and is defined wherever dq/d ln p is positive. Neighbouring fields already learn potentials this way. A learned free energy gives fluid properties as its derivatives, with exact Maxwell relations [L07, doi:10.1103/PhysRevE.105.045301]. In mixture thermodynamics, a graph network for the excess Gibbs energy replaced a Gibbs-Duhem penalty [L10, doi:10.1039/D3DD00103B] and needs no extra loss terms [L11, doi:10.1039/D4SC04554H], and hard-constraint networks build the Gibbs-Duhem relation into the architecture [L12, doi:10.1039/D4SC05115G] and take the excess enthalpy from the temperature derivative of a learned excess Gibbs energy [L13, doi:10.1038/s41467-026-71430-y]. In adsorption, a learned density functional has produced an isotherm of a Lennard-Jones fluid in a model pore [L41, arXiv:2608.13506]. In the sources we searched, no model learns such a potential from measured isotherms of real sorbents or tests it on water. <!-- lit_3a_ml.md section F, with its caveats: "not found in the sources searched"; ChemRxiv and L30 full text are blind spots -->

Hard constraints make a model admissible, but not necessarily accurate. For phase-transition enthalpies, a hard thermodynamic constraint raised the error by more than 10 kJ/mol, while a soft one kept the accuracy [L47, LAPSE:2025.0419]. Two parts of the construction can also act separately: the shape constraint fixes monotonicity and the Henry limit, while the temperature structure (a van't Hoff affinity per term, a free dependence on 1/T, or a characteristic curve) decides how the isotherm moves between temperatures. The open question is which of the two, if either, makes extrapolation in temperature better than penalties, generic networks and classical models.

We test this in a pre-registered factorial design that crosses the shape constraint (none, penalty or hard) with the temperature structure (free in 1/T, van't Hoff per term, or a temperature-invariant characteristic curve). The learned potential is the cell with hard constraints and van't Hoff terms.
